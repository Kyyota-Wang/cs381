Machine learning on observational health data: forecasting, survival models, and what a model conditioned on non-randomized exposure is and is not allowed to claim.

2026

A Patient World Model for Early Forecasting of Digital Health Campaign Outcomes: Capabilities and LimitsPreprint

Yunlong Wang

Advanced Analytics, IQVIA, Wayne, PA · arXiv preprint, September 2026

Read the PDF Cite

Abstract

Digital direct-to-consumer (DTC) health campaigns are usually measured after the fact. In-flight forecasting commonly relies on a separate classifier for every cutoff and horizon. We treat this task as a dynamic-system problem and build a compact patient world model. The architecture maintains a latent state per patient, learns exposure-conditioned state dynamics jointly with a weekly conversion hazard, and rolls forward into future conversion curves. We evaluate it on a US campaign dataset with 147,173 patients and 5.2 million at-risk person-weeks. In a retrospective evaluation conditioned on recorded future exposures, the model forecasts the remaining new-to-brand prescription volume through week 52 with a relative error of 2.9% from a week-4 cutoff and 0.8–2.6% from cutoffs at weeks 8–26. The strongest non-recurrent baseline, a pooled-hazard gradient boosting model given the same survival rollout, has relative errors of 13.6–33.1%. Per-horizon classifiers perform substantially worse. A Fisher-information analysis motivates dense next-exposure supervision when conversions are rare. Removing this auxiliary objective increases prescription-volume error by approximately 2–14×, while providing no consistent disadvantage on the more common specialist-visit outcome. We also evaluate scenario simulation. Switching all future exposure off raises predicted conversion from 0.31 to 0.89, a pattern consistent with selection effects in observational exposure data. This result highlights the limits of interpreting exposure-conditioned rollouts causally.

What the model is

  • A latent state per patient, advanced week by week by a sequence module — a two-layer GRU here, but the slot is swappable.
  • A survival hazard head that emits a weekly conversion hazard, and an auxiliary transition head that predicts next week's exposure summary.
  • A recursive survival rollout that multiplies weekly hazards into cumulative-incidence curves. They are monotone and consistent across every horizon by construction, which is what per-horizon classifiers cannot offer.
  • About 2×105 parameters, trained on CPU inside the certified environment the patient data cannot leave.

Table 1 of the paper. Relative error (%) of the forecast final new-to-brand prescription volume, from a cutoff at week τ through week 52, on the held-out test split with known future exposures. Coherent is the fraction of per-patient curves that never decrease. A perfectly calibrated forecaster still faces a 1σ sampling floor of 3.7%, 4.1%, 4.5% and 7.0% at τ = 4, 8, 13 and 26, so the world model's errors sit at or below the floor at every one of those cutoffs. At τ = 39 only 76 future converters remain and the floor rises to 11.5%, which is why that column cannot rank the coherent models and no conclusion is drawn from it.

Three things it shows

  • Structure, not capacity. Coherence and additive error propagation come from the survival rollout rather than from the network. Any pooled-hazard model with the rollout beats per-horizon classifiers by orders of magnitude.
  • Dense supervision earns its place when events are rare. A Fisher-information argument shows the outcome loss supplies roughly 1% of the encoder's gradient curvature at rare-event rates. Removing the transition head — one flag — costs 2–14× on prescription-volume error, and nothing on the ten-times-more-common specialist-visit outcome. The effect appears exactly where the analysis predicted it, and not elsewhere.
  • Scenario simulation has a hard limit. Within observed dose support the simulator behaves sanely. Switching all future exposure off raises predicted conversion from 0.31 to 0.89 — a selection artifact from outcome-dependent censoring, not a treatment effect. Exposure-conditioned rollouts must not be read causally.
Data and reproducibility. The underlying records are protected health information and stay on a secured analytics cluster; only aggregate metrics are reported, and the data cannot be shared. Cohort construction rules, feature definitions, hyperparameters and the full as-run result tables are in Appendices A–G of the paper, at a level of detail sufficient to rebuild the pipeline on comparable data. Model and evaluation code are being prepared for release.
Responsible use. This work forecasts observed health-seeking behavior under recorded digital exposure. It does not estimate causal effects. Outputs are decision support for campaign measurement and planning, not input to individual-level clinical or coverage decisions. The results come from one campaign, one therapeutic area and one country; external validity is untested.
